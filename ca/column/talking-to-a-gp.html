How to talk to a doctor about your foreskin

October 7, 2026

Knowing you want to ask about your foreskin is one thing. Finding the words in a small room with a doctor is another. Many people sit down, talk about something else, and leave without mentioning the real reason they came. That is very common. This article gives you some simple phrases you can borrow, a few ways to describe what you notice, and an idea of what usually happens once you have said it.

Key points

  • Plain, everyday words are fine to use
  • A few notes before you go can help you speak
  • You can ask questions and say what feels okay

Getting started: the first few words

The opening sentence is often the hardest part. Once it is out, the rest tends to follow. You do not need special language. Doctors hear about every part of the body, every day. Plain words are fine, and they are often clearer.

Here are some openings you could use, or adapt:

  • I would like to ask about my foreskin. It has been on my mind for a while.
  • This feels a bit awkward to say, but I have a question about my penis.
  • My foreskin feels tight and I wanted to check whether that is something to look at.
  • I have noticed a change down there and I would like someone to have a look.
  • I am not sure if this is normal, so I thought I should ask.

If you are booking by phone or online, you usually do not have to give details to the receptionist. You can say it is a personal matter, or simply that you would like to discuss a concern about your genitals. You can also ask whether you can see a male or female doctor if that matters to you.

If you freeze in the room

It is all right to pause. You could say, I am finding this hard to bring up. Many doctors will then gently help you along. Another option is to write your worry on a piece of paper or in your phone and simply show it. That counts as bringing it up.

Describing what you notice

A doctor will want to understand what you experience day to day. You do not have to know what it means. Your job is just to describe it as honestly as you can. A few notes made before the appointment can make this much easier.

  1. Write down what you notice, in your own words. For example: it feels tight when it draws back, or it does not go back fully.
  2. Note roughly when you first noticed it, and whether it has stayed the same or changed.
  3. Write down when it bothers you most, such as when washing, when passing urine, or at certain times of day.
  4. Mention anything else you have seen, such as redness, soreness, a smell, or any marks on the skin.
  5. List anything you have already tried, such as changing soap or washing routine.
  6. Add any questions you want answered, so you do not forget them.

Some useful phrases for describing things:

  • It feels tight when I gently draw it back.
  • It goes back part of the way, then stops.
  • It is uncomfortable rather than painful.
  • It has been like this for as long as I can remember.
  • This is new in the last few weeks.
  • Sometimes it looks red or feels sore after washing.

There is no wrong answer here. If you are unsure, saying I do not know is perfectly fine.

What to expect after you have explained

Every appointment is different, but most follow a similar pattern. Knowing the usual shape of it can make the whole thing feel more manageable.

Questions

The doctor will probably ask a few questions. These might be about how long you have noticed this, whether you have any pain, how passing urine feels, and about your general health. They are routine. They help the doctor build a picture.

A look, if needed

The doctor may ask whether they can take a look. They should explain what they would like to do first, and you can say yes or no. You can ask for a chaperone, which is another member of staff who stays in the room. You can also ask them to stop at any point. A look is usually brief.

Talking it through

Afterwards, the doctor will usually explain what they think and what the options are. That might be some general advice, a plan to check again later, or a referral to someone else. If anything is unclear, it is fine to ask them to say it again in simpler words.

Questions you might like to ask at this stage:

  • Is this something I need to keep an eye on?
  • Is there anything I should change about how I wash?
  • What should I look out for, and when should I come back?
  • Can you write that down for me?

When to talk to a doctor

You can talk to a family doctor about any foreskin worry, large or small. You do not need a reason that feels serious enough. Feeling unsure is reason enough to ask.

It is a good idea to make an appointment with a family doctor if you notice any of these:

  • Your foreskin feels tight and does not draw back comfortably.
  • Pain, soreness or stinging that does not settle after a few days.
  • Redness, swelling, a rash or skin that cracks or bleeds.
  • Discharge or a smell that does not go away with gentle washing.
  • Discomfort or changes when passing urine.
  • Any lump, mark or change that worries you.

Some situations need help straight away. If your foreskin has been drawn back and will not go back over the head of the penis, or if there is sudden, severe pain or swelling, do not wait for a routine appointment. Contact a medical service urgently. In an emergency, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. For health advice, most provinces offer 811.

If you are receiving treatment for the area, follow the advice you have been given and contact your family doctor if you have questions.

Summary

Bringing up a foreskin worry can feel awkward, but doctors are used to it, and plain words are fine. A short opening sentence is enough to begin. Simple notes about what you notice, when it started and what you want to ask can help you say what you mean. Expect a few questions, possibly a brief look with your agreement, and a conversation about next steps. You can ask questions, ask for a chaperone, and say what feels okay. If your foreskin will not go back, or there is sudden severe pain or swelling, get help straight away.

This article is general information, not medical advice or a diagnosis. If you have pain, swelling, bleeding or difficulty passing urine, talk to a doctor.
